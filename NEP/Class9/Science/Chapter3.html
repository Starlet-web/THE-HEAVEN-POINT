1. Introduction to Tissues

Tissue is a group of cells (similar in structure) that work together to perform a specific function.

In unicellular organisms, a single cell performs all functions of life. In multicellular organisms like plants and animals, different groups of cells perform different functions, leading to division of labour, which increases efficiency and enables complex life processes.

2. Plant Tissues

2.1 Meristematic Tissue

Meristematic tissues are found in the growing regions of the plant. They are groups of immature cells that are capable of division.

Figure 2.1: Diagrammatic representation showing the locations of apical, intercalary, and lateral meristematic tissues in plants.

Meristematic tissues are of three types:

Meristematic Tissue Location Function
Apical meristem Present at the growing tips of stems and roots To increase the length of stems and roots
Intercalary meristem Present at the base of leaves or internodes For the longitudinal growth of plants
Lateral meristem Present on the lateral sides of the stems and roots To increase the thickness of stems and roots

2.2 Permanent Tissues

Permanent tissue is formed from meristematic tissues. The cells lose the ability to divide. Permanent tissues are divided into two categories: Simple permanent and Complex permanent.

  • Simple permanent - Consist of only one type of cells performing same functions.
  • Types of simple permanent tissues: Parenchyma, Collenchyma and Sclerenchyma.

Key Characteristics of Simple Tissues

Parenchyma - Composed of unspecialized loosely packed living cells with relatively thin cell walls and large intercellular spaces.

  • Chlorenchyma: Parenchyma that contains chloroplast and performs photosynthesis is called chlorenchyma.
  • Aerenchyma: Parenchyma that contains large air cavities is called aerenchyma. These large air cavities provide buoyancy to aquatic plants.
  • Collenchyma - Composed of living and elongated cells with cell walls irregularly thickened at the corners; have very little intercellular spaces; provide flexibility and mechanical support to the various parts of the cells. They are present in leaf stalks just below the epidermis.
  • Sclerenchyma - Composed of long, narrow, and lignin deposited thick-walled cells. This tissue is made up of dead cells and there are no intercellular spaces. For example, husk of coconut.

2.3 Protective Tissues

The outermost layer of various parts of the plant such as the stem, roots, flowers, and leaves, transforms into protective tissues. The two types of protective tissues are epidermis and cork.

Epidermis

  • It is present on the outer surface of the entire plant body.
  • The cells of epidermal tissue form a continuous layer without any intercellular spaces.
  • The epidermis has minute openings in the leaves. These openings are called the stomata.
  • Each stoma is surrounded or enclosed by two kidney-shaped cells called the guard cells.
  • Transpiration also occurs through the stomata.
  • Epidermis is usually one-celled thick and is covered with a waterproof coating called cuticle.

Functions of Epidermis

  • It is a protective tissue of the plant body.
  • It protects the plant against mechanical injury.
  • It allows exchange of gases through the stomata.
  • Cuticle helps to reduce evaporation of water.

Cork

The outer protective layer or bark of a tree is known as cork. It is made up of dead cells.

  • Functions: It prevents loss of water by evaporation and protects the plant against mechanical injury, temperature extremes, etc.

2.4 Complex Permanent Tissues

These are made up of more than one type of cell. These tissues constitute vascular bundles. Types of complex permanent tissues are Xylem and Phloem.

Xylem

  • Conducts water and minerals from the roots to the different parts of the plant.
  • Composed of four different types of cells – Tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibres. Except xylem parenchyma, all other xylem elements are non-living.

Phloem

  • Conducts food material from the leaves to the different parts of the plant.
  • Composed of four different types of cells – Sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, and phloem fibres. Except for phloem fibres, all other phloem cells are living.

3. Animal Tissues

Animal tissues are classified into four types based on the functions they perform: Epithelial, Connective, Muscular and Nervous tissue.

Figure 3.1: Classification and structural overview of basic animal tissues.

3.1 Epithelial Tissues

Characterized by tightly packed cells with almost no intercellular spaces. It forms the covering of the external surfaces, internal cavities, and organs of the animal body. Various types include:

Squamous Epithelium

  • Single layer of extremely thin and flat cells are called simple squamous epithelium while multi-layered cells form stratified squamous epithelium in order to prevent wear and tear.
  • Location: Simple squamous is found in the lining of the mouth, esophagus, and lung alveoli. Stratified squamous epithelium is found in the skin.

Cuboidal Epithelium

  • Consists of cube-like cells that provide mechanical support.
  • Location: Lining of kidney tubules and ducts of the salivary glands.

Columnar Epithelium

  • Consists of elongated or column-like cells to facilitate movement across the epithelial barriers.
  • Location: Inner lining of the intestine and gut.

Glandular Epithelium

Consists of specialized multicellular glands that secrete substances at the epithelial surface.

3.2 Connective Tissues

These tissues are specialized to connect various body organs. Various types include:

Loose Connective Tissue

Includes areolar and adipose connective tissues.

  • Areolar Tissue: It provides support to internal organs and helps in repair of tissues. Found in the skin and muscles, around blood vessels, nerves, etc.
  • Adipose Tissue: Acts as the storage site of fats; found between the internal organs and below the skin; acts as an insulator for the body.

Dense Regular Connective Tissue

Main components are tendons and ligaments.

  • Ligaments: Connective tissues that connect a bone to a bone. It is very elastic.
  • Tendons: Connective tissue that connect a bone to a muscle. It has limited flexibility.

Specialized Connective Tissue

Includes structural skeletal tissues, i.e., cartilage and bone.

Fluid Connective Tissue

  • Blood: A special connective tissue present in animals that helps in the transport of various substances. Composed of plasma, Red blood cells (RBC), White blood cells (WBC), and platelets.
  • Lymph: A transparent, light yellowish fluid located in the intercellular spaces. It is part of the defensive mechanism and helps transport nutrients and digested fats from the intestine.

3.3 Muscular Tissues

Muscles, made up of muscular tissues, make it possible for the different body parts to move. Muscular tissues can be classified into three categories:

Striated Muscle Smooth Muscle Cardiac Muscle
Voluntary, striated Involuntary, unstriated Involuntary, striated

Striated Muscles

  • Show alternate light and dark bands or striations when stained appropriately.
  • Also called skeletal muscles because they are attached to the bones which form the skeleton.
  • They are voluntary muscles.
  • Cells are long, cylindrical, unbranched and multinucleate. Located in the body wall, tongue, limbs, and pharynx.

Smooth Muscles

  • Unstriated; do not show alternate light and dark bands.
  • They are involuntary muscles.
  • Cells are long with pointed ends (spindle-shaped) and uninucleate. Located in the alimentary canal, urinary bladder, blood vessels, and ducts of glands.

Cardiac Muscles

  • Muscles of the heart. Striated but involuntary in action.
  • They show rhythmic contraction and relaxation throughout life.
  • Cells are cylindrical, branched, and uninucleate.

3.4 Nervous Tissues

Highly specialized tissues present in the brain, spinal cord, and nerves.

Neurons

The fundamental unit of the nervous system is the nerve cell. Parts of neurons include:

  • Dendrite: Receives information from the axon of an adjacent neuron and conducts it toward the cell body.
  • Axon: Conducts messages away from the cell body.
  • Cell body: Contains a nucleus, mitochondria, and other organelles. Concerned with the maintenance and growth of the cell.

Schwann cells contain fat that forms the myelin sheath, which insulates neurons and speeds up signal transmission. Gaps in the sheath, called nodes of Ranvier, expose parts of the axon. At synapses, the axon endings of one neuron connect loosely to the next. Neurotransmitters released here carry the impulse across the gap, triggering a new impulse in the next neuron and continuing the signal to the brain.

Glial Cells & Nerves

Glial cells are supportive non-conducting cells in the nervous tissue. Nerves are bundles of axons enclosed in a sheath (Types: Sensory, Motor, Mixed).

4. Musculoskeletal System

The musculoskeletal system is made up of bones, muscles, joints, cartilage, tendons, and ligaments. This system helps us stand upright, move, maintain posture, and protect delicate organs. It functions under the control of the nervous system.

  • Muscles pull on bones to produce movement. They are attached to bones by strong, flexible bands called tendons.
  • When a muscle contracts, the tendon transmits this force to the bone, resulting in movement at a joint.
  • On average, the adult human skeleton makes up about 12 – 15 per cent of body weight, though this can vary with age, gender, and body composition.

Did You Know?

You can estimate your bone and muscle mass percentage using online references for your age and gender. For example, adult males have about 40–50% muscle, adult females ~30–40%, and bone mass is about 12–15% for all adults.

5. Skeletal System & Joints

5.1 The Skeletal System

The skeletal system consists of a framework of bones that provides strength and protects delicate internal organs. It includes the skull, vertebral column, and rib cage.

  • Skull: A hard case of flat bones joined by fixed joints, protecting the brain, eyes, and ears.
  • Vertebral column (spine): A flexible column made up of a series of small bones called vertebrae, with cartilage discs between each vertebra acting as cushions, allowing flexibility.
  • Rib cage: 12 pairs of ribs forming a protective cage around the heart and lungs. The ribs are attached to the spine at the back and to the breastbone (sternum) in the front by flexible cartilage, allowing expansion and contraction during breathing.

5.2 Types of Joints

A joint is a junction between two or more bones. Joints allow movement but they cannot move the bones on their own; muscles cause the movement.

  • Ball and Socket Joint: Example: Shoulder joint. Allows free movement in many directions. The rounded top of the upper arm bone fits into a shallow hollow of the shoulder bone.
  • Hinge Joint: Example: Elbow, knee. Allows movement in one direction only. The kneecap protects the knee joint.
  • Pivot Joint: Example: Neck (between skull and backbone). Allows side-to-side movement.
  • Fixed Joints: Example: Bones of the skull. No movement; protects the brain, eyes, and ears.

6. Totipotency and Biotechnology

Totipotency is the ability of a single cell to divide and differentiate into a complete organism. In 1958, F. C. Steward demonstrated that single phloem cells from carrot could regenerate whole plants.

  • He cultured phloem cells in a nutrient medium containing sugars and hormones, under appropriate conditions.
  • The cells dedifferentiated (regained ability to divide) to form an undifferentiated mass, which then redifferentiated into roots, shoots, and eventually a complete plant.
  • This shows that some mature plant cells are totipotent – similar to the ability of a zygote.

Meet a Scientist – Sipra Guha Mukherjee

Sipra Guha Mukherjee, in collaboration with S.C. Maheshwari, made a breakthrough discovery in plant tissue culture. Their research led to the development of a complete plant through anther culture using an artificial nutrient medium under controlled laboratory conditions. This work greatly contributed to crop improvement and modern agriculture.

Bridging Science and Society – Crown Gall Disease

In nature, Agrobacterium tumefaciens causes crown gall disease, leading to tumour-like swellings due to uncontrolled cell division. Scientists studied how this bacterium transfers genetic material into plant cells and later used this knowledge in genetic engineering. Today, Agrobacterium is used as a tool to introduce useful genes into plants for producing valuable phytochemicals, improved crops, and disease-resistant varieties.

Observations from Steward's Experiment

He found that light, air, and liquid medium with nutrients increased biomass, while absence of these factors reduced growth. This highlights the importance of environmental and nutritional conditions for totipotency.
