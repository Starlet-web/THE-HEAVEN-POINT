Ecology Levels

2. ECOLOGY is concerned with 4 levels of biological organization :

4 Levels of Biological Organization

3. POPULATION : Group of organisms of same species living in a well defined geographical area competing for similar resources and potentially interbreeding. (eg. Population of crows in Chandigarh)

COMMUNITY : All the populations of different species in a given area.

  • Ecosystem : It is a community along with the abiotic factors. (→ biotic + abiotic factors of an area)
  • Biome : A large distinct region with its characteristic vegetation, soil, climate, rainfall and temperature of desert, tundra, temperate forest etc.
  • BIOSPHERE : Part of Earth where life exists. It is the sum of all ecosystems and biomes.

Major Abiotic Factors

6. Major Abiotic Factors

  • a) Temperature (most imp)
  • b) Water (2nd most imp)
  • c) Light
  • d) Soil

1. Temperature

  • Temperature decreases progressively from equator to poles and from plains to high altitude.
  • In thermal springs and deep sea hydrothermal vents, temp. exceeds 100°C.
  • Temp. affects the enzymatic activity of the body.
  • EURYTHERMAL: Organisms that can tolerate a wide range of temperature.
  • STENOTHERMAL: Organisms that can tolerate only a narrow range of temperature.

8. Water

  • Life originated in water.
  • Aquatic organisms also face water-related issues like chemical composition, pH, and salinity of water.
  • Salinity in inland waters < 5 ppt (parts per thousand).
  • Salinity in sea is 30-35 ppt.
  • Hypersaline lagoons > 100 ppt.

9. Light

  • Plants - Photosynthesis requires light; Photoperiodism for flowering.
  • Animals - diurnal and seasonal rhythms; light is a cue for their mating, foraging, migrating.
  • Sun's energy/light do not reach deep oceans (> 500m).
  • Red algae are found in deepest waters out of red, green, and brown algae.

10. Soil

  • Nature of soil depends upon climate, the weathering process, whether the soil is transported or sedimentary, and how soil development occurred.
  • Various characters of soil: Soil composition, grain size, aggregation.
  • These characters determine the water holding capacity of the soil.
  • Types of soil: 1. Sandy 2. Clay 3. Loam.

Responses to Abiotic Factors

Responses: Conformers, Regulators, and Partial Regulators

12. Regulators

  • Maintain the internal environment of body irrespective of what is going on in external environment (maintain homeostasis).
  • All birds and mammals.
  • Very few lower vertebrates & invertebrates - Reason for the "success" of mammals.
  • Physiological regulation: sweating, shivering.
  • Behavioural regulation: migration.
  • Regulation is energetically very expensive.

13. Conformers

  • 99% of all animals; nearly all plants.
  • Thermoregulation is particularly very difficult for small animals (shrews and hummingbirds) as they have a larger surface area relative to their volumes.
  • Bergmann's rule: Size of populations and species becomes smaller in warmer areas and larger in colder areas. (i.e. small animals are rarely found in cold areas because they lose heat fast).

Adaptations

16. ADAPTATIONS : Any attribute of the organism (morphological, physiological, behavioural) that enables the organism to survive and reproduce in its habitat.

17. Physiological Adaptations

  • Kangaroo rat in North American desert: internal fat oxidation to release water, concentrated urine.
  • Desert plants → CAM cycle to preserve water; stomata remain closed during daytime.
  • Adaptation for high altitude (Altitude sickness): body compensates for low oxygen availability by increasing RBC production, decreasing binding affinity of Hb, and increasing breathing rate.

18. Behavioural Adaptation

  • Desert lizards bask in the sun and absorb heat when body temp drops, but move into shade when ambient temp starts increasing.
  • Some species burrow into soil to hide and escape from above-ground heat.

19. Morphological Adaptation

  • Desert plants: Thick cuticle on leaf surfaces, deep sunken stomata, leaves reduced to spines, stems flattened into phylloclades (Opuntia) to take over photosynthesis.
  • Aquatic mammals (Seals): Thick blubber (fat layer) below skin acting as an insulator to reduce body heat loss.
  • Allen's Rule: Mammals of colder climates generally have shorter ears and limbs to minimize heat loss.

Population Attributes

20. POPULATIONS generally consist of potentially interbreeding organisms (sexual reproduction), but a group of individuals resulting from asexual reproduction is also considered a population (e.g., Bacteria in a culture plate).

21. Population Attributes

I. Birth rate (b) - per capita births per unit time.

If 20 babies are born to a population of 100 people in a year:

b = $ \frac{\text{Births (B)}}{\text{Initial Population (N)}} = \frac{20}{100} = 0.2 $

II. Death rate (d) - per capita deaths per unit time.

d = $ \frac{\text{Mortality (D)}}{\text{Initial Population (N)}} $

III. Sex Ratio : Ratio/Percentage of males and females in a population.

IV. Age Pyramid : When age distribution is plotted for a population. Males are traditionally shown to the left and females to the right.

Age Pyramids representing Population Status

V. Population Density (N) : Population size is technically called Population density. It can be measured in terms of numbers, biomass, or percent cover.

Population Growth Models

Population Growth Equation

$N_{t+1}=N_t+[(B+I)-(D+E)]$

Under normal conditions, births and deaths are the most important factors. However, when a new habitat is being colonized, immigration may play a more important role than birth rates.

25. Exponential Growth (J-Shaped)

  • Assumes unlimited resource supply, no predation and no competition.
  • $\frac{dN}{dt}=(b-d)\times N$
  • Let $(b-d)=r$ (intrinsic rate of natural increase).
  • Therefore, $\frac{dN}{dt}=rN$.
  • Integral form: $N_t=N_0e^{rt}$.
  • Produces a J-shaped growth curve.
  • Population increases rapidly under unlimited resources.
  • Exponential growth is density-independent in this idealised model.
  • Examples: Bacterial growth under ideal conditions.
Exponential Population Growth (J-Curve)

26. Logistic Growth (S-Shaped / Sigmoid)

  • More realistic model of population growth.
  • Occurs when environmental resources are limited.
  • Carrying Capacity (K): Maximum number of individuals of a species that a habitat can support.
  • It is a density-dependent population growth model.
  • Population grows slowly (Lag Phase), rapidly (Acceleration Phase), then slows (Deceleration Phase) and finally stabilizes at carrying capacity (K).
  • Produces an S-shaped (Sigmoid) growth curve.
  • Intraspecific competition increases with population density.
  • Food, space and other resources act as limiting factors.
  • Environmental resistance prevents unlimited population growth.
  • At carrying capacity, population growth rate becomes zero.
  • In a stable population at K, birth rate approximately equals death rate (assuming no net migration).
  • Carrying capacity may change with environmental conditions.
  • Also called VERHULST-PEARL LOGISTIC GROWTH.
Logistic Population Growth (S-Curve)
Logistic Growth Equation

$\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(\frac{K-N}{K}\right)$

At N = K, $\frac{dN}{dt}=0$

Maximum growth rate occurs at N = K/2.

Maximum growth rate = rK/4

N = Population size | r = Intrinsic rate of natural increase | K = Carrying capacity

Population Interactions

There is absolutely no habitat on Earth where only a single species exists. Even a plant species needs soil microbes to break down organic matter and return inorganic nutrients. Thus, populations interact continuously.

Predation

  • Lion is a predator to deer. Sparrow is a predator to seeds. Herbivores are technically predators to plants.
  • Predators act as conduits for energy transfer across trophic levels.
  • Predators keep prey populations under control. Exotic species often become invasive due to the lack of a natural predator. (e.g., 1920s Australia: Prickly pear cactus spread rapidly until a cactus-feeding moth was introduced).
  • Biological control methods adopted in agriculture are based on the ability of predators to regulate prey population.
  • Predators help maintain species diversity by reducing the intensity of interspecific competition among prey species (e.g., Removal of Pisaster starfish caused extinction of 10 invertebrate species in a year).
  • Predators are "prudent" in nature (they don't overexploit prey to the point of prey extinction, as that would cause the predator's own extinction).

Defence Mechanisms

The problem of predation is particularly severe for plants since they cannot run away.

Competition

34. Competition: Process in which the fitness of one species ('r' value) gets significantly lower in the presence of another species.

  • Interspecific: Between 2 different species. (Darwin thought this is the most potent force in organic evolution).
  • Intraspecific: Among members of the same species. (Usually more severe).
  • Myth: Competition occurs only among closely related species. Truth: In shallow South American lakes, visiting flamingos and resident fishes compete for the same food (zooplankton).
  • Myth: Competition occurs only when resources are limited. Truth: In "Interference Competition", the feeding efficiency of one species is reduced due to the interfering and inhibitory presence of another species, even when resources are abundant.

Important Concepts

Gause's Exclusion Example: Abingdon tortoises in Galapagos Islands became extinct within a decade after goats were introduced on the island, due to greater browsing efficiency of goats.

Resource Partitioning Example: MacArthur showed that 5 closely related species of Warblers living on the same tree successfully avoided competition and coexisted by altering their foraging times and patterns.

Competitive Release Example (Connell's elegant field experiment): On the rocky sea coasts of Scotland, the larger and competitively superior barnacle Balanus dominates the intertidal area and excludes the smaller barnacle Chthamalus from that zone. When Balanus is removed, Chthamalus expands rapidly.

Herbivores and plants appear to be more adversely affected by competition than carnivores.

Parasitism

  • Parasitism has evolved in many taxonomic groups from plants to higher vertebrates.
  • Many parasites have evolved to be host-specific, such that both host and parasite tend to co-evolve.
  • Adaptations of Parasites: Loss of unnecessary sense organs, presence of adhesive organs/suckers, loss of digestive system, and high reproductive capacity.
  • Majority of parasites harm the host: they reduce the survival, growth, and reproductive rate of the host and make it physically weak.
  • (Female mosquito is not considered a parasite since it only takes a blood meal for reproduction, not for survival/habitat).

Commensalism & Mutualism

Commensalism (+ , 0)

  • Orchid & Mango: Orchid grows as an epiphyte on mango branch. Orchid gets space (+), Mango is unaffected (0).
  • Barnacles & Whale: Barnacles grow on the back of whales to move rapidly and get food (+), Whale unaffected (0).
  • Cattle Egret & Grazing Cattle: Egrets forage close to where cattle are grazing because the cattle stir up insects (+), Cattle unaffected (0).
  • Clown fish & Sea Anemone: Clown fish hides in stinging tentacles of anemone for protection (+), Anemone unaffected (0).

Mutualism (+ , +)

Both interacting species benefit. Shows tight Co-evolution.

  • Lichens: Intimate relationship between fungus (absorbs nutrients/water) and photosynthetic algae/cyanobacteria.
  • Mycorrhizae: Fungi in roots of higher plants. Fungi absorb phosphorus from soil; plant provides carbohydrates.
  • Fig and Fig Wasp: Highly specific. Wasp acts as pollinator and uses fig fruit as an oviposition (egg-laying) site, while larvae feed on seeds.
  • Yucca and Moth: Neither species can complete its life cycle without the other.

Sexual Deceit (Pseudocopulation)

The Mediterranean orchid Ophrys employs sexual deceit. One petal of its flower bears an uncanny resemblance to the female of a specific bee species in size, color, and markings. The male bee gets attracted to it, 'pseudocopulates' with the flower, and gets dusted with pollen. When it repeats this with another flower, pollination is achieved.
